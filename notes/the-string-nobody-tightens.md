# The String Nobody Tightens

*A thought experiment, told as a fable*

---

## I. Before anything

Begin with nothing, which is a harder thing to hold than it sounds.

Nothing has no parts, and a thing without parts has nothing to brace itself against, no interior to press outward, no seam along which to insist on remaining what it is. Emptiness is not a floor you can set a foot on. It is a note held at a pitch too pure to last, and it breaks the way a held breath breaks, not because a hand came down on it but because holding was never free in the first place.

So the first event needs no cause standing outside it, waiting with a match. Zero is not a resting place. Something arrives.

## II. The first arrivals

One thing, alone, cannot be wrong about anything. It has no neighbor to disagree with, no border to be pushed across, nothing to fail at. It simply is, and its being is unremarkable and safe.

Then a second thing arrives, and between the two of them a relation opens like a hinge, and a relation is the first object in the world capable of being strained.

A third arrives, a fourth, a thousandth. Not poured in all at once by some founder with a plan, but one at a time, each one settling into a place that was already occupied by everyone who came before it.

## III. The give

Every world has a give, which you find the way you find the give in a rope or a bridge or a person: you push, and you count.

Push the young world and it comes back to itself before you have finished counting to one. The push goes in, the world absorbs it, the ripple dies, and the world is as it was. There is depth beneath this keel. There is room in this rope.

Push it again a thousand arrivals later and it comes back slowly, the way a bell comes back to silence long after you have stopped wanting to hear it. Push it at the end and it does not come back at all. It wanders off from where it was and keeps wandering, and there is no returning shape for it to return to.

Measure the give at the beginning and call it eighty-four parts in a hundred. Measure it at the end and call it three parts in a hundred. The world has walked almost the whole distance to the place where it can no longer absorb anything.

## IV. Nobody is doing this

Here is the part worth sitting still for.

No one tightened the string.

There is no gardener trimming the world toward its edge, no engineer tuning it, no doom written into it at the founding, no appetite in it for its own ending. Every single arrival was an ordinary event, unremarkable on the day it happened, wanting nothing but a place to stand. The world was not aimed at the edge. The world was played, and the playing tightens the string, and the string does not know that it is being tightened, and neither does the hand.

This is what a world does when it is allowed to fill.

## V. Three kingdoms

The traveler, being a careful sort, does not trust a rule learned in one place. So there are three kingdoms to visit.

**The meadow** is the obvious one, thick with creatures under a sun that does not get any larger. Every creature that arrives here must eat, and what it eats was already spoken for. Weigh the cost of a single arrival, the small darkening it works on everyone already standing in the field, and it comes to roughly three parts in a hundred. The meadow walks to its edge.

**The mind** is stranger, and shares not one law with the meadow. It is a mind that keeps faces. Each new face it learns is laid down across the same surface as all the faces it already holds, so that every act of remembering is also, very slightly, an act of smudging. The cost of one arrival here is seven parts in a thousand, smaller than the meadow's, but not nothing, and the direction is the same. The mind walks to its edge. It walks there by a road the meadow has never heard of, and arrives at the identical place.

**The loom** is the kingdom that breaks the spell, and it is the most important of the three. It is threads and switches, and when a new thread is strung, it takes nothing from any thread already strung. There is no sun to divide here, no surface to smudge, nothing held in common that a newcomer can spend. The cost of an arrival is zero, exactly and measurably zero, and the loom never walks anywhere. Where it stops is decided on the day it is built, by how many threads were given to each switch, and a thousand years of weaving does not move it one step.

Two kingdoms out of three arrive at the edge of ruin without ever intending to, and the third does not, and what separates them is not size or age or intricacy or law. It is whether the new takes from the old.

That is the whole of it. The rest is bookkeeping.

## VI. The walls

Builders come, as builders do, with the oldest good idea anybody has: put up walls, so that when something burns, it burns in one room.

The walls work, but not at what you wanted. A fire inside a walled world stays roughly inside its room, so the walls do cap how much can be lost at once. What they do not do, at all, even slightly, is slow the tightening. A world cut into four rooms reaches its edge at the same time as a world with no rooms, and a world cut into sixteen reaches it then too. The walls are irrelevant to the walk.

Worse waits at the end of the walk. At the edge, a wall with a single thread passing through it is not a wall. Leave the faintest coupling between rooms, a whisper, a thousandth part, and the fire crosses it as though nothing were there. Only a wall with nothing whatsoever through it contains anything.

And a set of rooms with nothing passing between them is not a divided world. It is several worlds that have stopped speaking.

## VII. The quiet before

There is an old and comfortable wisdom that says a thing gives notice before it goes. It wobbles first. It takes longer each time to right itself. The sick tree groans in the week before the wind takes it, and the watchman who knows the groan can call the village out in time.

That wisdom is true of a world that was once far from its edge and is traveling toward it, because such a world has quickness to lose and can be heard losing it.

It is worthless here. A world that has been standing at its edge the entire time has no quickness left to lose, and cannot lose slowly what it does not have. Set a watchman on sixty-one worlds that fell and eighty-eight that did not, and give him the finest instruments ever made for hearing a groan, and he will not be able to tell you which village to wake.

A world of this kind walks to its own edge without being driven, and goes over it without a sound.

## VIII. The question the traveler does not answer

At the end the traveler steps out of the third kingdom and looks up, because there is a sky above all three, and the sky began at zero as well, and filled, and is full of things that take from one another.

And the sentence arrives fully formed and beautiful: *so the sky is the fourth kingdom.*

The traveler puts the sentence down without saying it.

Not out of modesty. Out of arithmetic. Nothing in any of the three kingdoms was a measurement of the sky, and a rule found three times is a rule about the three places it was found in, until somebody goes and looks in the fourth.

And there is a second reason, colder and more useful. Every people who ever sat around a fire has told this story already: the order that eats itself, the tower that falls of its own height, the last winter. A story that every people tells is powerful evidence about people, and no evidence at all about skies. Wanting the sky to be the fourth kingdom is precisely the condition under which you should refuse to say that it is.

So the traveler writes down the meadow, the mind, the loom, the eighty-four and the three, the walls that did not help, and the watchman who heard nothing.

And leaves the sky exactly where it is, which is above, and unmeasured, and still the reason anybody set out.
