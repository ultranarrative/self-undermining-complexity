Reproducing May 1972, and testing one prediction against it

Will a large complex system be stable?

Robert May asked it in Nature in 1972 and answered with a threshold. Wire together S species at random, let each interaction have typical strength σ and let a fraction C of the possible links exist, and the community holds together only while σ√(SC) stays below 1. Past that, it falls apart. Drag the controls and watch the threshold arrive.

Three things this rig was built to settle

The bound reproduces

Sweeping σ√(SC) across 150 draws per point, the probability of stability collapses at 1.092 for S = 50, 1.036 at 100 and 1.026 at 200. It converges on 1 from above as the finite-size wobble shrinks, which is what May predicted.

Compartments do not move the threshold

Hold the link budget fixed and redistribute links inward, and the transition sits at 1.045 with no compartments and 0.972 when they are sealed. Full separation is marginally worse. Packing the same links into smaller blocks raises the density inside each one by exactly enough to cancel the size reduction, so the eigenvalue disk keeps its radius. Set m to 4 and drag q to see the cloud refuse to shrink.

What they do instead is cap the damage, and only when sealed

Press one species near the boundary and the worst case moves 91 percent of an integrated community against 24 percent of a compartmentalized one, which is exactly the share inside one compartment. The surprise sits at q = 0.99: with roughly 15 links left of 1500, damage still reaches 69 percent. Partial separation is not partial containment.

Method. May's original community matrix, with -1 on the diagonal and off-diagonal entries drawn independently from N(0, σ²) with probability C. Eigenvalues are computed in the browser by Householder reduction to Hessenberg form followed by Francis double-shift QR, checked against LAPACK to 1.2e-14.
